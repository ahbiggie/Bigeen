Understand the workflow before you automate it

Software can make a sound process faster. It can also make a poor one more expensive, harder to change and easier to ignore.

Bigeen Solutions 28 September 2026 2 min read

When operations start to strain, a new system is often the first response: a tracking tool, a customer database, a dashboard, an app. The instinct is reasonable. Technology can remove repetitive work, connect information and make performance visible.

But a tool is only as useful as the workflow it supports. When the workflow itself is unclear, the new system tends to inherit the problem rather than solve it.

What automation actually does

Automating a process means encoding it: its steps, rules, handoffs and exceptions become fixed in software. That is valuable when the process is sound. When it is not, one of two things usually happens. Either the software faithfully reproduces the confusion, or it imposes a process that nobody actually agreed to — and people work around it.

Automation does not decide how the work should be done. It fixes whatever answer you already have in place.

Familiar patterns

  • The tool that duplicates work. The new system goes live, but the old spreadsheet stays, because the system does not quite match how the work is really done.
  • The dashboard nobody trusts. The reporting looks good, but the underlying data is entered inconsistently, so decisions still rely on someone’s memory.
  • The system built around one person. It was configured to suit whoever led the project, and makes less sense to everyone else.
  • The workaround that comes back. An exception the system cannot handle is managed by email or phone, and gradually becomes the real process again.

Questions to answer first

  1. What is the work, step by step, as it actually happens today?
  2. Who owns each step, and who makes each decision?
  3. Where does information enter, and who relies on it later?
  4. What has to be true for the output to be right?
  5. What should change about the process itself, not just the tool?

These questions are not a delay before the “real” work. They are the design work that makes the technology worth building.

Design the two together

Process design and technology design are best treated as one piece of work. Decisions about steps, roles, controls and data should be made alongside decisions about the tools that will carry them, and tested with the people who will use them.

Starting small helps: automate one well-understood part of the workflow, check that it produces the result it was meant to, and extend from there. A system that fits the work is far easier to grow than one the work has to be bent around.
