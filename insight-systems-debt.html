Systems debt: how organisations outgrow the way they work

Most organisations do not decide to run on informal processes and individual memory. They drift there, one reasonable shortcut at a time.

Bigeen Solutions 28 September 2026 3 min read

When an organisation is small, informality works. A handful of people share the same context. Decisions happen in conversation, knowledge sits with the people doing the work, and problems are fixed by whoever notices them first. That is not a failure. At that size, it is often the most efficient way to operate.

Growth changes the arithmetic. More people, more customers, more handoffs, more services, sometimes more locations. The ways of working that once held the organisation together were never designed for that load, and they start to strain.

What systems debt is

We use the term systems debt for the gap between how an organisation now needs to work and how it actually works. Like technical debt in software, it is the accumulated cost of shortcuts that made sense at the time they were taken.

Nobody chooses it. Each workaround solves a real problem. A spreadsheet fills a gap a process never covered. An experienced colleague becomes the person everyone asks. A new tool is added to fix a symptom. Individually these are sensible. Together they form an operating system nobody designed.

How it builds up

It tends to show up in the same few ways:

  • Processes remain informal. Work gets done, but the way it gets done lives in habit rather than in anything that can be taught, checked or improved.
  • Work becomes dependent on individuals. A few people hold the knowledge that keeps things moving. When they are busy, away or leave, work slows or stops.
  • Documentation falls behind reality. What is written down describes how things worked two years ago, so people stop consulting it.
  • Management loses visibility. Leaders are asked to make decisions without reliable information about what is actually happening.
  • Technology is introduced without fixing the workflow. New tools inherit the old confusion, and often add a layer of their own.
  • People operate inside systems that were not designed for them. Staff compensate with extra effort, and the effort becomes invisible.

Why it is hard to see

Systems debt rarely announces itself. It accumulates gradually, so there is no single moment when things obviously break. The people closest to the work absorb the strain: they remember the exceptions, chase the missing information and fix the errors before anyone else notices.

That effort hides the problem. Leaders see symptoms that seem unrelated: a missed deadline, a quality issue, a frustrated customer, an exhausted member of staff. Each is treated on its own, and the pattern underneath stays in place.

The effort people put into compensating for weak systems is exactly what keeps those systems from being noticed.

Paying it down

Reducing systems debt rarely calls for a dramatic overhaul. It calls for deliberate, connected changes:

  1. Make the actual work visible. Map who does what, where work is handed off, and what is written down versus only remembered.
  2. Find where the strain concentrates. Handoffs, single points of knowledge and decisions made without information are usually where the debt is heaviest.
  3. Change the connected parts together. A process change without matching roles, documentation and tools tends not to hold.
  4. Build the capability to sustain it. The people who run the new way of working need to understand it, own it and be able to improve it.
  5. Measure whether it is working. Decide in advance what should be different, and check.

A question to start with

If the person who knows most about a critical part of your operation were away for a month, what would stop? The answer is usually a good map of where the debt sits.
